Does ethnic cleansing facilitate economic development by increasing local homogeneity, or undermine it by destroying existing diversity?

We address this question by analyzing the local **economic** and **political consequences** of ethnic cleansing over time. 

Combining village-level data on ethnic cleansing with satellite images of **nightlight emissions** across 18 Sub-Saharan African countries (1992–2021),
we find that ethnic cleansing initially reduces economic activity in some contexts, but is generally followed by **recovery** above pre-treatment levels. 

These effects are mostly driven by **state-affiliated perpetrators** of ethnic cleansing. In addition, ethnic cleansing is associated with increased government support among co-ethnics of perpetrators, but less political engagement among targeted groups. 

We test the mechanisms behind this **“two-sided”** effect with case evidence from Nigeria, Uganda, and Mali. Our results show that ethnic cleansing increases **local ethnic homogeneity** and state infrastructure in affected areas, which may contribute to economic recovery and government support among perpetrators' co-ethnics. 

By highlighting that ethnic cleansing may deepen **economic** and **political inequalities** between ethnic groups, our findings have important policy implications for humanitarian aid, development, and peacebuilding.
