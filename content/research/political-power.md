Although UN peacekeepers frequently operate in ethnically divided societies, existing research has paid limited attention to how **ethnic inequalities** shape peacekeepers’ ability to **protect civilians**. 

I address this gap and argue that perpetrators with access to **state power** can constrain peacekeepers’ ability to protect marginalized ethnic communities. 

Combining geo-located peacekeeping deployments with data on **ethnic one-sided violence** across UN missions from 1999 to 2020, I find that peacekeepers **reduce** violence by politically **marginalized perpetrators** against
groups represented in government, but not violence perpetrated by politically powerful groups.

These findings identify ethnic **power inequalities** as a key constraint on international civilian protection. 
Such limitations may reinforce existing inequalities in post-conflict societies and highlight
the importance of **local-level reconciliation** efforts, including intergroup dialogue and mediation.
